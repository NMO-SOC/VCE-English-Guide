Section B Under Exam Conditions

On this page
  • Rules and Myths
  • Stay on the Framework
  • Recent Exam Titles
  • Forms That Have Worked
  • Craft Reminders

In the SAC you had around 80 minutes, a known prompt, teacher consultation and a written commentary. In the exam you have about 60 minutes, an unseen title, unseen stimuli and no commentary. Everything about purpose, audience and context must come through the writing itself.

A Reading-Time Process

  1. Turn only to your own Framework. The other three are a distraction.

  2. Cover the visual stimulus. Start with the title, because the title, not the image, is the core of the task.

  3. Unpack the title: what does it imply? Which aspects of your Framework does it open up?

  4. Now read all three stimuli, including the visual. Which one adds a spark that extends, deepens or broadens what the title has suggested?

  5. Choose the form and approach you have practised that best suits this title.

Rules and Myths

  • The title is fixed. You cannot change it or add to it. Write it at the top of your response. If your shaded box and your title disagree, assessors follow the title.

  • Choosing a title chooses its Framework. No mixing titles, Frameworks or stimulus sets.

  • The title should be threaded through the whole piece, internalised as its controlling idea. You need not repeat the exact words, though you may.

  • The stimulus is used at least once and interpreted freely. You may read an image in opposite ways (is the tape being put on, or peeled off?) so long as your reading is clear. Use the idea the stimulus generates, not its literal contents: a tree in the image does not require a tree in your piece.

  • More stimuli earn no extra marks. One, two or three is your choice. There is no correlation between number used and score.

  • There is no preferred form. Anything except poetry, song or verse, and you may embed a line of poetry or a lyric inside prose.

  • Do not write a story about the Framework. Narrative elements are fine, but the piece must convey ideas, not just an engaging plot.

  • Crossing out a word to improve it is a strength, not an error.

Stay on the Framework

The most damaging mistake is a fine piece that has drifted off the Framework. Two common versions:

  • Protest as a verb instead of a noun. The Framework explores protest as a concept: why it arises, why it fails, its consequences, its moral high ground. A piece that simply rants about something you are angry about is not about protest.

  • Touching the Framework once. A piece about something else, with one scene of children playing in a park, is not about play. The ideas must be Framework ideas from start to finish.

Thin ideas also cost marks. “Protest is good” and “people should play more” are unexamined. Explore causes, consequences and limits.

Forms That Have Worked

Examples published in recent assessment reports show how far form can flex:

  • An advertorial for a play-based childcare centre (Play, 2024), aimed at parents of preschoolers, with a distinct marketing voice and the title used explicitly.

  • A recipe for protest (Protest, 2024), using imperatives, varied sentence length, subheadings, historical awareness and a touch of humour.

  • A sectioned reflection on “Finding My Way” (Personal Journeys, 2024), with four subheadings (chameleon, metamorphosis, mosaic, reminisce), each marking a stage of development.

These are not templates to copy. They show that form should be chosen to sharpen ideas.

Craft Reminders

  • Fluid and hybrid forms. Blend styles: an opinion piece with a narrative opening, a speech that quotes a song lyric.

  • Subheadings organise ideas and signal a deliberate structure, and they can mark shifts in time or place.

  • Paragraphs are your choice. Vary length and number for effect; one paragraph is one extended idea.

  • Watch heavy dialogue. Long stretches of colloquial first-person dialogue tend to flatten complex ideas.

  • Consider audience and context even though you cannot state them: who is reading, when and where, and what do they already believe?

  • Practise unseen. Every practice piece from now should use a title you have not seen before, timed at 60 minutes, following the reading-time process above.
