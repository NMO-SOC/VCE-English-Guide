Exam Mindset, Timing and FAQs

How Your Paper Is Marked

Each section is marked by at least two assessors, each giving a score out of 10, combined into a mark out of 20. If the two scores are too far apart, a third (or further) assessor reads the response. Assessors mark holistically against the expected qualities, and always in relation to the topic or title. They read every word and reward as far as they can. In 2025 the average for each section sat around 5.4 to 5.5 out of 10.

Attitude Is a Predictor

An Australian study of students under test conditions, controlling for ability, found that attitude alone predicted results. “Confident striders” (prepared, and trusting that the test would be fair) did best; students who expected the test to be unfair and stressful tended to fulfil that prophecy. Practical takeaways:

  • Trust the paper to be fair. Recent papers have been. A question may be phrased unexpectedly, but you are transferring skills you have practised to a new instance.

  • A little nerves is useful. Adrenaline speeds thinking. Worrying about things you cannot control only takes up brain space.

  • Confidence comes from preparation, not from hoping.

Habits of Successful Students

  • Train for three hours. Do some study in three-hour blocks so the exam is not the first time you concentrate that long. Work out what breakfast keeps you going.

  • Practise, get feedback, act on it, practise again.

  • Eat well, sleep, and exercise to burn off anxiety when it tips from useful to unhelpful.

  • Have a backup plan. No single exam decides your future. Missing a first-choice course often means a transfer a year later.

Time Discipline

The three sections are worth equal marks, so give each one hour. The first marks in any response are the easiest to earn. An extra half hour polishing one essay gains far less than the same half hour spent on the next. Use the room clock, and when the hour is up, move on.

Plan, Then Write

Planning is not extra work; it shifts the work. When you write without a plan, you are deciding what to say and how to say it at the same time, and your brain switches between the two and loses time. Plan first (only you need to read it), check it is relevant to the topic, then give your full attention to expression. Fluency is assessed in every section.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • How many quotes should I use in Section A? There is no number. It depends on how many other kinds of evidence you use (structure, character, setting, language features, context), and every quote must be explained, not just included.

  • Should I memorise a strong practice response? No, in any section. You cannot know the topic, title or text, and a memorised piece that does not answer the question cannot score highly.

  • Which section should I do first? Personal choice. Some students do A, B, C in order; others start with whichever they plan fastest. Try different orders in practice exams.

  • What should I do the night before? Put the books away and sleep. A year of preparation will not be changed by two more hours.

  • What if I make a mistake? Cross it out neatly and keep going. If you realise mid-essay you have drifted, fix your topic sentences, but stay within your hour. Checking your plan early is the best prevention.

  • What if I don’t get the score I want? You get the score you get. It is a snapshot of one day, and your skills keep growing after it.
