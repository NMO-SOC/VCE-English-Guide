Analysing Stage Directions

On this page
  • A Worked Ladder: The Blue Jewels
  • Directions Worth Knowing Closely
  • Stage Direction Evidence Bank

In a play, the author speaks twice: once through the dialogue, and once through everything the audience is shown. Harrison’s stage directions – lighting changes, props, physical business, silences – are deliberate authorial constructions, which makes them premium evidence in a Section A essay: quoting one instantly signals that you are treating the text as something made. The rule, however, is absolute: you are never writing about stage directions. You are writing about character, relationships, ideas, concerns and values – and using stage directions as evidence to prove your interpretation. Identifying a direction and stopping is the mid-band trap; the marks live in what it proves.

The Evidence Ladder

Before a stage direction goes anywhere near your essay, climb it through five rungs: What is the direction? What does the audience literally see? What does that reveal? What idea, concern or value does it support? And finally – what does Harrison want the audience to understand? Consider the difference in these three sentences about the moment Dolly and Errol look at each other “longingly”: saying “Harrison uses the stage direction ‘longingly’” merely identifies; saying it “shows that Dolly and Errol like each other” merely explains; but writing that “Harrison’s direction that the pair look at each other ‘longingly’ allows their developing affection to be communicated visually, positioning their relationship as a source of emotional possibility” analyses – it moves from choice to meaning to purpose. Reach for precise analytical verbs as you climb: constructs, establishes, reveals, emphasises, reinforces, contrasts, symbolises, positions, foregrounds, intensifies, challenges.

A Worked Ladder: The Blue Jewels

Take the direction from Gladys’s dream in Act 1, Scene 7: “A tap appears from nowhere and from it flows blue jewels in an approximation of water.” What we see: water presented as treasure. What it reveals: the intensity of Gladys’s longing for something most of the audience has never once thought about. What idea it supports: dignity, housing and inequality – the politics of the play made visible in a single image. What Harrison wants understood: that the ordinary becomes precious only when it has been withheld, so the audience is made to feel the family’s deprivation rather than merely be told about it. Built into a paragraph, the ladder becomes: argument (Gladys’s hopes are shaped by material deprivation), evidence (the “blue jewels”), analysis (the theatrical image renders running water magical), significance (the depth of her longing for basic security), link (what many audiences regard as ordinary becomes a powerful aspiration for those living without it).

Directions Worth Knowing Closely

Some of the play’s directions carry entire arguments within them. The Prologue’s opening – “gloomy outside”, everything “sodden and mud-splattered” – makes the setting perform the family’s circumstances before a word is spoken, while the women’s calm, practised rebuilding tells the audience this catastrophe is routine: Harrison stages normalised crisis, not singular disaster. The Inspection’s “nervous, anxious energy” as Nan and Gladys straighten everything in sight physicalises the power the welfare state holds over the household – fear expressed through housework. At the bank, the manager “pours a cup for himself, but doesn’t offer one to GLADYS”: four seconds of stage business that dramatise an entire social hierarchy without one hostile word. At the ball, “DOLLY is frozen in shame” – stillness as violence, the visual opposite of her “swirling and floating” entrance moments earlier; the two directions analysed together map her humiliation more precisely than any dialogue. When “their humpy disappears” to the sound of bulldozers, Harrison erases the family home as an act of theatre, and the replacement housing rises “concrete, small, white and featureless” – the adjective “white” doing quiet, double work. And in Act 2, Errol finds Gladys “in a similar scene to the first time he saw her, chopping wood” – a structural echo that measures how far the relationship has travelled by holding the setting still. Small kindnesses are staged too: Errol “notices GLADYS is sitting on a kero tin so he does the same” – respect performed as posture, the visual antithesis of the bank manager’s tea.
