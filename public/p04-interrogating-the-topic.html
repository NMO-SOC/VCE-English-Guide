Interrogating the Topic

On this page
  • Two Kinds of Quotation Topic
  • Ordering Is Not Connecting
  • Concepts Worth Rehearsing

Every Section A topic is new, and every topic is more than a single concept. The exam will never ask “tell me everything the author says about punishment”. It will modify the concept, by asking about its causes, its consequences, its impact, or its relationship with another idea. Reading time is where you take the topic apart before you commit to a contention. (Adapted from the VATE 2026 student revision lectures.)

Questions to Ask of Every Topic

  • Absolutes. Look for words such as all, no one, only, always. Is the claim true for everyone in the text? If not, who is the exception, and why (class, gender, race, belief, family)?

  • Modifiers. Adverbs and adjectives change the question. “Punished” and “justly punished” are different essays. “Moments” and “lasting, ordinary moments” are different essays. Define each modifier before you plan.

  • Connotation. “Fear that is justified” is not “irrational fear”. “Infatuation with youth” implies shallowness and impermanence that “love of youth” does not.

  • Comparison. Most, more than, as guilty as, strongest: these words require you to weigh one thing against another, not discuss one thing alone.

  • Connections. “Change is inevitable” implies nothing can stay the same. “Interplay” means two ideas act on each other, not a one-way cause.

  • The verb. Celebrates, condemns, warns, mocks each tell you the author’s attitude. Do not flatten them into “presents”.

Two Kinds of Quotation Topic

Quotation topics do not all work the same way, so read them closely.

  • The quote as a key moment. The quote is attached to a question (“How is the idea of truth explored?”). The panel has chosen a passage that matters to the question. Decide where it fits in your sequence of ideas and discuss it there.

  • The quote as the proposition. The quote is the claim you are asked to assess (“To what extent does the author demonstrate this to be true?”). Here the quote is the topic. Treating it as one piece of evidence would mean missing the question entirely.

Ordering Is Not Connecting

“Firstly”, “secondly”, “initially”, “subsequently” put paragraphs in order. They do not explain how one idea relates to the next. A connection says why paragraph two follows paragraph one: it builds on it, qualifies it, complicates it, or shows its consequence. Before you write, check two things in your plan: that every topic sentence answers the topic, and that you can state the link between each pair of paragraphs in one phrase.

Concepts Worth Rehearsing

Recent Section A topics have centred on concepts such as hardship, home, control, social convention, threat, knowledge, punishment, truth, choice and privilege. The panel can and does introduce new ones. A useful revision routine: take each concept, ask what your text says about it, then ask what it says about the concept’s causes, its consequences, and its relationship to a second concept. You build knowledge and practise the skill at the same time.
