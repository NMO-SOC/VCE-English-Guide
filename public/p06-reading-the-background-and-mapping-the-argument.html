Reading the Background and Mapping the Argument

Section C tests three skills applied to a text you have never seen: inferential reading, analytical thinking and clear expression. The text itself is deliberately easy to comprehend. What is hard is reading it inferentially.

What You Already Know About Persuasion

Persuasion happens when a reader trusts the writer, sees the issue from a new angle, believes they share the writer’s values, and understands what part they will play in the proposal. So in every text you can expect the writer, at some point, to:

  • establish their credibility;

  • present the status quo as needing change (or as worth protecting);

  • reframe how the reader understands the issue;

  • present the proposal as consistent with the reader’s values;

  • show how it serves both the individual and the common good.

You do not know how they will do these things. That is what you discover.

Comprehension Is Not Enough

Comprehension questions (Who wrote it? How long has the event run?) are Year 7 tasks. Inferential questions are yours:

  • Who is persuading whom, of what?

  • What do these readers believe and value? Infer it from the publication, the community and the history given.

  • What is the writer’s relationship to the issue? An unaligned “local resident” seems impartial and credible. The chair of an organisation that will close has a stake and must work harder against suspicion.

  • What are the stakes? Asking readers to “consider” change one day is low stakes. Asking them to vote tonight for something they dislike is high stakes, and the writer’s strategy will reflect that.

State these findings briefly in your introduction, so you can draw on them throughout.

Two Recent Contrasts

  • 2025: a resident proposes that a regional town replace a 50-year fireworks tradition with a light show. Tradition is comforting and change is hard, so he spends most of the piece building a case for change, and the stakes are low (“some time in the future”).

  • 2024: the chair of a struggling community gym proposes leasing the foyer to a lolly shop. Her readers built the gym for health, so the proposal clashes with their values, and she needs a vote. High stakes, invested speaker.

How Arguments Open

Openings are strategic building blocks. Three common moves (not the only ones):

  • outline a situation and frame it as a problem the writer will solve;

  • establish the writer’s credibility first, so later claims are trusted;

  • establish shared values first, so the reader feels on the same side.

A writer who wants a tradition abandoned may open by praising it. That is not a contradiction: shared affection earns the trust needed for what comes after the but.

Watch the Hinge Words

But, however, yet, until: what follows a hinge is usually the writer’s real point. Mark every hinge as you read. They often reveal the argument’s structure, for example a repeated pattern of problem, hinge, then a better alternative.

Explain the Sequence, Do Not Just List It

“Then”, “next” and “second” only order the argument. Explain the link instead.

  • Weak: Next, the writer reframes the status quo as problematic and uses targeted examples to make the audience see the situation differently.

  • Strong: Having established shared values with his readers, the writer turns those same values against the fireworks, moving systematically through care for the environment, fairness to vulnerable residents, the welfare of pets and economic responsibility. This relentless return to the readers’ own values makes change seem not a loss but the only choice consistent with who they are.

Choose the Most Powerful Techniques

Almost every persuasive text appeals to values, characterises its subjects positively or negatively, and compares the favoured option with the alternative. Beyond those, techniques vary each year. You cannot analyse everything, so choose the elements used most powerfully in this text, whether common or unusual. A popular technique such as inclusive pronouns may be the least important thing on the page.

Pace Yourself Through the Text

A common error is spending too long on the opening and running out of time before the argument’s turn or conclusion. Plan your selections across the whole text before you write.

Analyse the Example, Not the Technique

“The writer uses statistics to support their view. This gives the argument credibility” would fit any text, so it analyses nothing. Analyse this example: “Citing figures from the AMA implies thorough research, and the before-and-after comparison, showing participation doubled, presents the intervention as clearly effective.” If your analysis of an example takes only a sentence, check that you are explaining the example rather than defining the technique.

What, How, Why

Every analytical point needs three parts: what the writer is doing, how (the specific words or image), and why these readers, with these values, are likely to respond. Summarising what was said is the “what” alone. It is not wrong, but it is incomplete. As you improve, the three parts can merge in a single fluent sentence; the bulk of a strong paragraph is the how and the why.

The Visual

  • Describe versus explain. “The image shows a small dog hiding under a sheet” is description. Explaining shows how the dog’s small size and cowering posture amplify the written claim that pets are innocent and vulnerable.

  • Analyse it where it belongs. The visual is part of the text. Analyse it alongside the argument it supports, so you show how it is integrated. Tacking it on at the end shows less skill, but is far better than forgetting it.

  • Tick it off. Put a tick beside the visual in the task book once you have written about it.

Do Not Evaluate

The specifications state you are not required to evaluate whether the argument succeeds. Judgements such as “this is effective” waste time and earn nothing. Explain how the persuasion works and why the intended readers would respond, not how people in general would respond.
